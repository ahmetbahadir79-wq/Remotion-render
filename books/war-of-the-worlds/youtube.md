The Martians don't lose to our armies. They lose to a germ — and Wells did that on purpose.

That's the key to "The War of the Worlds" by H.G. Wells, and it's not the alien-invasion story you were told. In this deep dive we make the case that this isn't a monster book at all — it's a mirror. Wells takes the exact logic Victorian England used to justify its empire — that superior intelligence has the right to erase "inferior" life — and turns it on London. The Martians aren't evil. They are us, seen from the losing end. And the ending is a deliberate humiliation: humanity does nothing to win. We are saved by the smallest thing alive.

We trace the whole argument through the book's real scenes — the microscope in the opening lines, Wells naming the extermination of the Tasmanians, the white flag vaporized at Horsell Common, the Heat-Ray and the Black Smoke, the Thunder Child, the curate, the artilleryman's fantasy, the red weed, and the bacteria that end it all. Then we sit honestly with the objection: is the germ ending a cheat, and does Wells really humble the ladder or just reorder it?
